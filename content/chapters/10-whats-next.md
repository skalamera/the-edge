You now understand AI better than most people who talk about it for a living. Plenty of executives can name the tools. Very few can explain why a model hallucinates, why every answer costs money, or which layer of the stack a headline is really about. You can.

The hard part now is keeping up. This chapter gives you four things: the trends worth tracking, a one-minute filter for any announcement, a fifteen-minute daily system for staying current, and a ready-made talk you can give.

## Seven trends worth tracking

These aren't predictions. They're lines already moving. For each one: what's happening, what's real versus promised, and why you should care.

### 1. Agents are working longer on their own

The old question was "can AI answer this?" The new one is "how long can AI work before a human has to step in?"

A research nonprofit called METR tracks exactly that: the length of task, measured in human working time, that AI [[agent|agents]] can finish about half the time. That length doubled roughly every seven months from 2019 to 2025, and closer to every four months since 2023. By early 2026, the best models could handle software tasks that take a skilled person several hours.

**Real vs. promised:** Most of those tasks are coding, which is well defined and easy to check. Messy work, full of politics, judgment and missing context, lags well behind. Watch for agents that take a multi-step job overnight and hand you a finished draft in the morning.

### 2. Intelligence keeps getting cheaper

The price of a given level of AI performance keeps collapsing. Epoch AI, an independent research group, estimated in September 2026 that it has fallen roughly 13x a year since 2023, faster than the historical price declines of computer chips, batteries or DNA sequencing. Treat the exact figure loosely. The direction is not in doubt.

**Why it matters:** A feature that was too expensive to run on every bet, every play or every customer last year may be cheap this year. Total AI spending still rises, because cheaper capability gets used far more. For contracts, think twice before locking in today's per-unit price for three years.

### 3. AI moves onto your devices

Not every request needs a giant data center. Phones, laptops, glasses and earbuds now ship with chips built to run smaller models locally. [[on-device ai|On-device AI]] is faster (no round trip to the cloud), more private (the data stays with you) and carries no per-request cloud bill.

**Real vs. promised:** The most capable models still live in the cloud, so the pattern is hybrid: easy tasks on the device, hard ones sent up to the cloud. In a stadium, or on a second screen during a live game where every second counts, that split matters.

### 4. Physical AI: cars first, robots later

[[physical ai|Physical AI]] means models that see and act in the real world. The clearest proof is robotaxis. By 2026, Waymo was giving roughly half a million paid driverless rides a week across a growing list of US cities. That's real.

Humanoid robots are the opposite case. The demos are spectacular, but deployment is still pilots, not fleets. When you see a robot folding laundry on video, ask how many are doing paid work today.

**In your world:** Robotic cameras, [[computer vision]] tracking and automated production are the nearer-term versions. Same technology family, pointed at the field of play.

### 5. AI starts doing science

The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went in part to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of Google DeepMind for AlphaFold, an AI system that predicts the 3D shapes of proteins. That was a turning point: AI didn't just summarize science, it advanced it. The labs now pitch their models as research partners in math, biology and materials.

**Why it matters:** This is the labs' strongest argument for their enormous spending, and the claim most worth checking. There's a worked example in the next section.

### 6. Voice becomes the interface

Typing into a chat box is a transitional habit. Voice mode already works well in the major assistants, and hardware makers are betting on glasses, earbuds and new devices built around talking. OpenAI has said it's building a device with former Apple designer Jony Ive, reportedly not shipping before 2027.

**In your world:** Picture a fan asking a sportsbook app, out loud and mid-game, for a bet on the next drive. That's a product opportunity, a [[latency]] challenge and a responsible-gaming question all at once. Regulators will care how a conversational interface nudges behavior.

### 7. AI-native companies with tiny teams

A new kind of company is being built with AI in every workflow from day one. Lovable, a Swedish startup whose product builds apps from plain-English descriptions, said in March 2026 that it had passed $400 million in annual recurring revenue with about 146 employees.

**Why it matters:** Your next competitor, partner or acquisition target may be twenty people. In due diligence, revenue per employee becomes a headline metric, but so does revenue quality: usage-based AI revenue can arrive fast and leave just as fast. Inside big companies, ask the sharper question: what would this team look like if we built it today?

## What stays constant: the one-minute filter

Every week brings a new model, a new record on some [[benchmark]], and a funding round with a number that sounds made up. You don't need to track them all. You need a filter.

Here's the good news: the fundamentals move much more slowly than the news. Training still costs a fortune up front, and [[inference]] still costs money every time. Models still predict, so they still [[hallucination|hallucinate]]. Proprietary data and distribution are still the durable [[moat|moats]]. Anything that hasn't been measured with real [[evals]] is still a demo. Those ideas will outlast every model name you hear this year.

:::analogy Sharp money or public money?
When a line moves, a good trader doesn't react to the move. They ask what caused it. Sharp money from bettors with a real edge? News, like a late scratch? Or public money piling onto a popular team? Same move, very different meaning.

AI announcements are line moves. A product shipping to paying customers, independent test results, a big customer renewing: that's sharp money. A slick demo, a benchmark the company chose and graded itself, a giant funding round: that's public money. It tells you about excitement, not capability. Your job is to know which one you're looking at before you move.
:::

The filter is five questions. Ask them in order.

1. **Which layer is this?** Energy, chips, cloud, models, tools or apps (chapter 4). The layer tells you who makes money and who gets squeezed.
2. **Real or demo?** Is it shipping? To whom, and at what price? "Available to select partners" and "coming later this year" both mean *not yet*.
3. **Who checked?** Was the result verified by someone with no stake in it, or graded by the company that built it?
4. **Who pays whom?** Many "partnerships" are really customer contracts, investments or cloud credits in disguise.
5. **What changes for us?** If this is true, what would a sportsbook, a league, a media partner or your own team do differently in the next 12 months? If the answer is "nothing yet," file it and move on.

### A worked example

In September 2026, OpenAI announced that its AI agents had solved a version of the Navier-Stokes problem. It's one of the Millennium Prize Problems, a famous set of open math questions that each carry a $1 million prize. Run the filter:

- **Real or demo?** Real work, not a staged video. Experts could examine the math.
- **Who checked?** Within two weeks, two disputes broke out. One over credit: researchers working with AI on a simpler version accused OpenAI of building on their work without proper credit, which OpenAI denied. One over substance: several mathematicians argued that the version solved relies on an artificial outside force, making it far less meaningful than the version the field cares about.
- **The calm read:** AI doing serious, original mathematics is a real and remarkable step. "AI solves one of math's greatest problems" oversold it. Both things are true at once.

That's the posture that earns credibility. You're impressed by what's real, unmoved by the headline, and willing to wait a week for the experts.

:::myth Myth vs. reality
**Myth:** Miss a month of AI news and you're hopelessly behind.
**Reality:** The headlines change weekly; the fundamentals in this book change slowly. If you understand the stack, the economics and the failure modes, you can absorb a month of news in an hour. If you only read headlines, you're always behind.
:::

## Your system for staying current

Willpower fades. Systems hold. This one takes about fifteen minutes a day, plus a few extra on Mondays.

### Monday: This Week in The Edge

Every week, this playbook publishes a short update in the This Week section: the handful of developments that actually mattered, each run through the filter above and linked to the chapter it builds on. If you read nothing else, read this: Monday morning, before your first meeting.

### Daily: the 15-minute habit

- **5 minutes of input.** One podcast segment on the drive, or one newsletter with your coffee. Not a feed. Not a scroll.
- **10 minutes of use.** Try the thing you just read about, on a real task. This is what separates people who know *about* AI from people who know AI.

### The short list

You don't need twenty sources. You need a few good ones and permission to ignore the rest. Every source below is well established and was publishing actively as of September 2026.

**Pick one daily listen**
- **The AI Daily Brief** (podcast, host Nathaniel Whittemore). About 20 minutes a day on what happened and why it matters. Built for the commute.

**Pick two weekly reads or listens**
- **Hard Fork** (New York Times podcast, Kevin Roose and Casey Newton). The smartest general-audience tech show going. Good for flights.
- **One Useful Thing** (newsletter, Ethan Mollick). A Wharton professor on how to actually use AI at work and in the classroom. The closest match to your own two jobs.
- **Stratechery** (Ben Thompson). The business strategy of tech: who wins, who pays, and why. Weekly articles are free; the daily analysis is paid, and the bundle even includes an NBA podcast.
- **The Batch** (free weekly newsletter from Andrew Ng's DeepLearning.AI). Calm and clear, from one of the field's most respected teachers.

**For a long flight**
- **Dwarkesh Podcast.** Long-form interviews with lab leaders and researchers. The best way to hear how the people building this actually think.

**Bookmark for the numbers**
- **Stanford AI Index** (every spring). The standard reference for data on AI progress, investment and adoption. Cite it in lectures.
- **Benedict Evans's presentations** (twice a year, free on his site). A veteran tech analyst's big-picture deck on where the industry is heading.
- **Epoch AI.** Independent charts on compute, costs and trends. Check a number here before you repeat it.

Keep reading the sports-business trades you already read. They tell you what the industry announced. These sources help you judge whether it matters.

Two more habits. **Go to the primary source:** when a lab announces something, read its own post, not someone's post about the post. And **follow the earnings calls:** each quarter, Nvidia and the big cloud companies reveal more about the AI economy through their spending plans than a month of headlines will.

What to ignore: viral threads promising "10 prompts that will change your life," any headline announcing that AGI has arrived, and anything you've only seen in a screenshot. Give big claims 72 hours. The corrections usually arrive by then.

:::room Say this in the room
- "Before we react: which layer of the stack is this, and who's actually paying whom?"
- "The demo is impressive. What does it look like on our messy data, at our volume?"
- "Let's give this a week. The independent numbers usually show up after the press release."
- "If the next model release can do this out of the box, what's left of the vendor's advantage?"
:::

## Become the teacher

The fastest way to lock in what you know is to teach it. Explaining forces you to find the gaps in your own understanding. You already have a classroom, and a company full of people who want AI explained in their language.

Two formats, same bones.

### The 45-minute lunch-and-learn

| Time | Segment | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Hook | A live demo on a real but non-confidential task, like summarizing a public league report. Let them see it work, and let them see it slip. |
| 5–15 | How it works | Next-token prediction, training vs. inference, why it hallucinates. Chapter 3 in ten minutes. |
| 15–22 | The money | The stack, and why proprietary data is a moat. |
| 22–30 | Chatbots to agents | What agents do well today, and where they break. |
| 30–38 | Our industry | Three examples from betting, data and media. Pose company-specific ideas as questions, not plans. |
| 38–42 | Rules of the road | What never goes into a consumer AI tool. Always verify. |
| 42–45 | The assignment | "Use it on one real task every day this week. Bring back one win and one failure." |

Your handout is the Cheat Sheet, the next chapter.

### The 75-minute guest lecture

Same spine, plus a case discussion. Two that work well with sports-industry students:

- **The data-rights debate.** Should a league license its official data to an AI lab for training? What should it charge, and what should it never allow? Split the room into league, lab and sportsbook, and let them negotiate.
- **The sponsorship audit.** Computer vision can now measure every second a logo is on screen. Does that make sponsorship valuation more honest, or just more complicated? Who should own that measurement?

End with an assignment that makes students use the tool critically: have AI draft a sponsorship proposal, then grade its assumptions.

### Three rules for teaching AI

1. **Show, then explain.** A two-minute live demo beats twenty slides.
2. **Say "nobody knows" out loud.** On timelines, jobs and AGI, honest uncertainty is more credible than a confident forecast.
3. **Date your numbers.** "As of this fall" protects you when the figure changes. It will.

:::teach Teach it in 60 seconds
Open with a hook: "Every week, an AI headline says everything just changed, and it's almost never true." The key idea is to treat an AI announcement like a line move: before you react, ask whether it's sharp money (a product shipping to paying customers, checked by independent experts) or public money (a demo and a press release). In September 2026, headlines said AI had solved one of math's most famous problems. Within two weeks, mathematicians were arguing over whether it had solved the version that matters. The capability was real, and the headline still oversold it. Your edge isn't reading more news; it's asking better questions of the news you read.
:::

## The edge compounds

In betting, the truest measure of skill isn't whether you won last night. It's whether you consistently got a better number than the line closed at. Beat the close by a little, again and again, and the edge compounds. No single bet makes you. The habit does.

AI fluency works the same way. You don't need to predict the future. Nobody can, including the people building it. You just need to be a little better informed than the room, every week. Fifteen minutes a day. One Monday update. One new thing tried. One idea explained to someone else. Six months from now, those small edges have stacked up, and you're the person people call when AI comes up.

You picked up this playbook because you saw a chance to get ahead. That window is still open, but it narrows as more people catch up. The next moves are simple:

- **Today:** choose your daily listen and your two weekly sources, and subscribe.
- **This week:** use AI on one real task every day, and read This Week on Monday.
- **This month:** give the lunch-and-learn. Put it on the calendar now, before you feel ready. Teaching is how you get ready.

The edge isn't what you know today. It's the habit that keeps you ahead tomorrow. Start today.

## Check yourself

1. A partner forwards a press release: a startup says its betting AI "outperforms human traders" on a new benchmark. Using the filter, what do you ask first?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Start with "who checked?" If the company built and graded the benchmark itself, that's public money, not sharp money. Then "real or demo?": is it live with paying sportsbooks, at real volume and latency? Finally, "what changes for us?": what does it cost per decision at scale, and who carries the risk when it's wrong?

</details>

2. You're evaluating a potential partner: an AI-native startup with 25 employees and fast-growing revenue. What does the small team change about your due diligence?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Headcount no longer tells you much about scale, so dig into revenue quality. Is revenue usage-based and likely to churn? Does it depend on one model provider whose prices or terms could change? Is the moat proprietary data and distribution, or a thin layer on someone else's model that the next release could copy? Key-person risk also rises when a few people run everything.

</details>

3. You've been too busy to follow AI news for a month. What's your one-hour catch-up plan, and why is an hour enough?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Read the last four This Week updates, skim the recent issues of one weekly source, run anything big through the five-question filter, then try the one new capability that matters most to your work. An hour is enough because the fundamentals haven't moved. You're updating details, not relearning the game.

</details>
